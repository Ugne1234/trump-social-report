Built for the base: what 17 years of Trump posts teach politicians about social media in 2026

Trump's feed is very good at keeping his most loyal supporters engaged. It is much worse at holding the newer voters who put him back in office. Here is what works, what flops, and what other politicians should copy and avoid.

Covers @realDonaldTrump on Twitter (May 2009–Jan 2021) and Truth Social (Feb 2022–Apr 2026), 89,325 posts collected; 59,566 of his own posts analysed. Plus outside polling and platform data up to November 2026. By Ugne.

01Raw likes measure fame, not what works

Ranking posts by likes mostly tells you when they were posted. His median tweet got 79 likes in mid-2013 and about 165,000 in November 2020. Any "top posts" list built on raw counts is really a list of his most famous months.

So every post in this report is scored on engagement lift: its likes compared with the median like count of his posts from the surrounding 30 days. A lift of 2× means twice the likes of a typical post at that point in his career, which takes his follower growth (and Truth Social's smaller audience) out of the comparison. We built a small Python tool, postlift, to do this consistently across both platforms; the code is supplied with this submission (see Method).

02What the base rewards: emotion, conflict and stakes

We modelled engagement lift on what each post is about, how it is written, its format and its timing, all at once, so each bar shows that factor's own effect. Trump's Truth Social audience is the closest thing in public data to his committed base: the platform is small and built around his own following.

Three patterns stand out. The first two hold on both platforms, a decade apart:

  • Heat beats light. Exclamation marks (+27% on Truth Social), insults and nicknames (+9%) and ALL-CAPS (+3% for every 10 points of a post in capitals) all lift engagement. His nicknames are dependable performers: "Sleepy/Crooked Joe" posts average 1.5× a typical post, and "Crooked Hillary" and "Fake News" posts average 1.3×.
  • High stakes and a clear enemy. War and foreign policy (+15%), faith and culture-war themes (+8%), crime (+4%) and election-fraud claims (+2%) outperform. The labels supplied with the dataset tell the same story: posts tagged as a Threat average 1.66× and Denials 1.4×, while Praise barely beats a typical post (1.0×).
  • Say something with substance. On Truth Social, posts of 41–80 words get about a third more engagement than one-liners of 15 words or fewer. The punchy one-line tweet of 2016 is not what this audience rewards now.

Three checks. First, the pattern is not an artefact of counting likes: models of shares, replies and total engagement give nearly identical results (coefficients correlate at 0.96–1.00 with the likes model), and replies reward conflict even more strongly (insults +32%, naming opponents +17%), which matters now that feeds weight replies and shares heavily. Dropping the most extreme low posts shrinks the effects a little (exclamation +21%, endorsements −49%) but changes none of the conclusions. Second, some of "emotion works" is really "his own voice works". In 2015–17, tweets from Trump's own Android phone averaged 1.24× a typical post, against 0.95× for tweets from staff iPhones, and carried nearly three times as many insults. In a simple model, allowing for the device roughly halves the exclamation effect on Twitter (from +6.7% to +3.5%).

Third, the theme findings do not hinge on the keyword coder. On a random sample of 600 Truth Social posts from 2022–26, coded for the four themes the argument rests on by an LLM (Claude) reading the codebook, the model points the same way. War and foreign policy: +24% (95% CI +8% to +41%), against +21% with keywords on the same posts. Endorsements: −64% against −69%. The economy and naming opponents stay close to zero, with intervals spanning zero, as in the full data. Treat this as a consistency check, not independent validation: 600 posts gives wide intervals, and the LLM is the same model that helped build this analysis and had seen the hand-coder's notes.

What barely moves the base: the economy. Posts about jobs, prices and trade do only slightly better than average on Truth Social (+4%, about a quarter of the lift for war and foreign policy), and 7% below average on Twitter. In the simple comparison below, second-term economy posts look strong (1.37×), but mostly because they tend to be long, substantive text posts. One shift stands out. In his second term, most substantive themes outperform his average text post by 1.3–1.5×. Part of the reason is that so much of his output is now low-engagement filler (endorsements, reposted clips, bare links), which drags the average down. The substance still lands; it is increasingly buried.

Endorsements are dead weight. His "Complete and Total Endorsement" posts use a fixed template listing the candidate's stance on the border, the economy and the Second Amendment. Handing the audience somewhere else costs reach. Bare links get 26% less engagement than a typical post, and text posts containing an outside link get about 13% less. This cannot just be blamed on algorithms: links underperformed on Twitter in 2015–21, long before Elon Musk confirmed X was throttling them in 2024. X now says it stopped that penalty more than a year ago, but in this data the cost of links comes from audience behaviour as much as code. Reposted clips and media with no comment also underperform (about 0.9×).

Volume buys reach, not attention. On his busiest days (41 or more posts; the record is 138 in a single day) each post averages 0.7× typical engagement, against 1.6× on days with 1–5 posts. But total engagement still rises with volume, from roughly 5 typical posts' worth on a quiet day to roughly 44 on a busy one. The per-post fall is mostly housekeeping: 65% of posts on 41+ days are endorsements, reposted media or bare links, against 29% on quiet days, although even his own substantive text posts slip (1.7× to 1.1×). So posting more can add reach (the Senate Democrats report 430% engagement growth from posting more, a self-reported figure), but every low-value post lowers the return on the rest.

04Moments beat messages

The biggest outliers in every era are not clever lines. They are moments when Trump is the news: declaring his candidacy, his COVID diagnosis, the Butler assassination attempt, the Israel–Iran ceasefire, a drug-price announcement. A single post at the right moment can earn 10 to 40-plus times a normal post. Typical weak posts are housekeeping and hand-offs: quoted headlines, courtesy messages, congratulations, endorsements and merchandise.

Under-performers are posts near the bottom 5% of each era, not the extreme minimum: like counts are snapshots taken when the data was collected, and the very lowest values cannot be verified (one widely shared December 2020 tweet is recorded at 19,000 likes when that week's median was about 224,000).

05The coalition gap: what the feed rewards isn't what the coalition needs

Trump's 2024 win depended on new groups: men under 50, whom he narrowly won after losing them by 10 points in 2020, and Hispanic voters, who split 51–48 for Harris after favouring Biden 61–36. According to AP VoteCast, the economy and jobs were these voters' top issue.

His own feed rewards something else. Economy posts get only modest extra engagement from his core audience, while conflict, war and grievance content outperforms. A feed tuned to its most engaged users drifts towards what the base wants. The polling shows the newest supporters are the ones drifting away:

To be clear, the polling shows where the gap is, not that posts caused it: falling approval among Hispanic and younger supporters is far more plausibly about prices, tariffs and deportations than about posting style. The point is narrower: a feed tuned to the base gives these voters little reason to stay engaged. Trump's team appears to know this. NPR's analysis shows he regularly reposts clips taken from X and TikTok onto Truth Social, and the campaign's own figures put its 2025 growth on TikTok, Instagram and Facebook rather than Truth Social. Truth Social is where the base is kept engaged; the persuadable coalition has to be reached on other platforms.

What this shows. Trump's party copied his targets but not his voice. While he was president, Republican members almost never named Democrats (about 1% of posts in 2017–18). Once they were in opposition, a quarter of their posts did. But their capitals, insults and exclamation marks stayed exactly where Democrats' are, at roughly a fifth of Trump's level, every quarter for six years. Whether that was the weaker half depends on the platform. On Twitter, where this comparison sits, naming opponents earned about as much as his tone did (opponents +7%, exclamation +8%, insults +4%). On Truth Social today, tone pays far more (exclamation +27%, insults +9%, opponents +4%). Either way, targets are the part of Trump's style that is easiest to copy without sounding like him. They also talk about different things: members of Congress post far more about health care and far less about elections (2% vs Trump's 8%) and legal fights (2–3% vs 9%).

Public evidence from 2024–26 suggests the voice can travel across parties when someone deliberately adopts it, and that the Trump campaign itself reached beyond Truth Social: in 2024 it toured long-form podcasts popular with young men, such as Theo Von's and Joe Rogan's.

Democrat · CaliforniaGavin Newsom: copying the style

In August 2025 his press office began parodying Trump's all-caps style, nicknames and sign-offs. ABC7 reported the account gained 250,000 followers in August alone, with an engagement rate above 50%. Emotion and conflict work for both parties. One media claim, that Trump then cut back on capitals, is not borne out in the weeks after the parody; his capitals did fall from December 2025, which we cannot attribute to it (see the chart below).

Democrat · New York CityZohran Mamdani: video, not posts

His 2025 campaign was built on short, personal, multilingual videos about everyday costs. His Instagram videos drew 43–55% of their views from non-followers, which is the kind of reach beyond the base that Truth Social cannot offer.

Democrat · New YorkAlexandria Ocasio-Cortez: a long-standing personal brand

Her personal accounts (over 12m followers on X and 9.5m on Instagram) far outstrip her official ones. As with Trump, people follow the person, not the office.

Republican · Vice PresidentJD Vance: writing his own posts

Vance reportedly writes his own posts and leans into the memes made about him; his October 2025 Halloween post parodying them topped 15 million views, according to Fox News. That fits our Android finding: in Trump's data, his own voice outperformed staff-written posts.

07Seven lessons for politicians in 2026

  1. Be the news, not a commentator on it. Announcements and real moments earn 10–40× a typical post. Reposted headlines and bare links earn less than a typical post.
  2. Write with feeling and stakes. Emotional punctuation, a clear opponent and plain language are rewarded on both platforms. Most of Congress copied Trump's targets but not his tone; on Truth Social today, tone is the stronger driver. Newsom showed in 2025 that the tone works for Democrats too. Attention and persuasion are not the same thing, though (lesson 6).
  3. Don't spend the main feed on housekeeping. Endorsements, merchandise and fundraising asks are the worst performers. Batch them, or move them to separate channels and email.
  4. Treat volume as a reach tool, and keep filler out of it. Busy days add total engagement, but each post earns less (1.6× per post on quiet days, 0.7× on days with 41 or more), mostly because housekeeping takes over.
  5. Have something to say. On today's Truth Social, substantive 40–80-word posts beat one-liners. Platforms in 2026 reward time spent on a post, not drive-by slogans.
  6. Don't mistake base engagement for coalition support. The content that engages the base most (grievance, conflict) is not what the newer groups in the coalition prioritised (the economy), and those groups are the ones drifting away. Measure reach beyond your followers, not just likes.
  7. Use a different platform for each job. Use the owned platform to mobilise the base. Use short video, podcasts and creators on TikTok, YouTube and Instagram to reach persuadable voters. That is where Trump's own team found its 2025 growth.

08What we'd offer ADI next

This report answers ADI's questions with the data available today. These five follow-on projects would close the gaps it flags itself:

LLM theme coding for all 60,000 postsIn a pilot, an LLM matched hand codes far more closely than keywords (F1 0.92 vs 0.66, an upper bound). Rerun every theme finding on that coding, with monthly human spot checks.
An always-on trackerRun the postlift pipeline on Trump's feed every week: engagement lift, topic mix, endorsement share and the week's biggest over- and under-performers, published as a dashboard.
TikTok and YouTubeExtend postlift to the platforms where persuadable voters actually are, using views and non-follower reach rather than likes.
A survey linking engagement to voter groupsShow real posts to a sample of voters, to turn this report's "group link: inference" labels into measured effects.
A pre-flight checker for ADI's own commsCalibrate the post checker on several politicians, so ADI can test its own messages before they go out.

One timely question for the tracker: endorsements were the weakest content in our data and took over his feed before the 2026 midterms. By NewsNation's count, at least 11 of the 22 governor candidates and 9 of the 24 Senate candidates he endorsed lost on 3 November. Our data cannot say whether his posting played any part; linking endorsement posts to results race by race is a natural next step.

Method in brief

  • Measure: each post is scored on engagement lift, its likes compared with Trump's typical post in the surrounding 30 days, so results aren't distorted by his audience growing about 2,000-fold. Effects come from regression models of lift on topic, style, format, length and timing together.
  • Data: 59,566 of Trump's own posts (from 89,325 supplied tweets and Truth Social posts; reposts, replies, fan retweets, deleted and zero-like posts removed), plus 105,312 posts by 403 members of Congress for the comparison.
  • Limits: likes measure the reaction of people who already follow him, not persuasion. Themes are coded by keyword; checked against 200 hand-coded posts, the keywords scored 70% precision and 62% recall, so theme effects are probably understated. The data shows correlation, not causation.
  • Method and AI use: the tool, data decisions, checks and AI use are set out in the one-page method note.
